Riprap, open-source flood and heat evidence for New York City

The flood and heat record for any New York City block, cited line by line.

Riprap joins street sensors, 311 complaints, flood maps and storm records for an address or community district into one page. For heat it reads satellite surface temperature, tree canopy, the Health Department's Heat Vulnerability Index and the Weather Service forecast. Rules or an open Granite model read your question and choose the evidence. Every sentence you read comes word for word from a public record, with its source and date.

Runs in seconds on a laptop: no GPU, no API keys.

Open models, open problems

I fine-tuned open models for New York and tested each against a simple baseline. One, which maps paved, green and tree-covered land, is in briefings, labelled experimental, with its tested accuracy in every sentence. One, a surge forecast, lost to its baseline and was taken out. The results are published either way.

  • Paved, green and tree canopy

    Experimental

    Estimates how much of each 10 m pixel is tree canopy, grass, paving or roof, in 2018, 2021, 2024 and 2026. A TerraMind fine-tune, trained on the city's 2017 six-inch map. A briefing quotes that map first, and the model's estimate follows it.

    Measured
    On squares it never trained on, it reads a typical district's paved share 1.7 points above The Nature Conservancy's 2021 map. Its citywide tree canopy reads 4.2 to 5.0 points below the city's 2017 map in three of its four yearly maps.
    The open problem
    Maps of different summers differ by more than two images of one summer, so it cannot yet show change between years. Telling real change from the images is the next result to earn.

  • Storm surge at the Battery

    Negative result, off by default

    Forecasts how far the water may run above the predicted tide over the next four days. A Granite TTM r2 fine-tune. It is out of default briefings since 2026-10-05.

    Measured
    On 639 held-out four-day windows from January 2025 to October 2026, its mean error was 0.115 m. Damped persistence, a one-line rule, scored 0.108 m and beats it. The tide table alone scored 0.167 m.
    The open problem
    It foresaw 1 of the 23 windows that reached flood stage, which are 5 distinct events. A forecast that a simple rule beats adds nothing to the Weather Service forecast a briefing already quotes. The model card on Hugging Face is out of date; the corrected card is the one linked here.

A third model, a satellite water layer, was retired on 2026-10-02 after two tests, one on Hurricane Ida and one on coastal and tidal floods that FloodNet sensors recorded at the moment of a satellite pass: in both it found recorded floods no more often than chance.

Built on 34 public sources

City, state and federal records, read live or from their published files and sorted into five Stones: 25 for a flood briefing and 11 for a heat briefing, two of them shared. No commercial data and no scores. Each record has its own portal; Riprap reads them together for one place and links back to each.

Hazard maps and storm records Cornerstone
FEMA's effective and preliminary flood maps, the city's four modelled stormwater flood maps for today, 2050 and 2080, the 2012 Sandy inundation extent, USGS Hurricane Ida high-water marks, USGS terrain For heat: Landsat surface temperature, the NYC Health Department's Heat Vulnerability Index and heat illness emergency visits, the city's 2017 land cover map (tree canopy)
Places at risk Keystone
NYC public schools, MTA subway entrances, NYCHA developments, hospitals, NYC Planning's floodplain counts; DOB construction permits only when a question asks For heat: NYC Parks spray showers and pools
Live sensors and complaints Touchstone
FloodNet street sensors, NYC 311 complaints about flooding and sewer backups, NOAA tide gauges, USGS stream gauges, National Weather Service observations For heat: Weather station records of 90 degree days, the latest air temperature at the nearest station
Forecasts and projections Lodestone
National Weather Service alerts and water-level forecasts, NPCC4 sea-level projections For heat: The National Weather Service forecast and heat alerts, NPCC4 heat projections
How answers are written Capstone
Code turns each record into one cited sentence, and rules set the answer's lead (Yes, No, a count). An optional open Granite model may route a question the rules do not match and choose among those sentences; it never writes one, and code checks its choice.

FloodNet data is licensed CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 and the NPCC4 projections CC-BY-NC 4.0; the other sources are public domain or open. See every source and licence.

For developers and AI agents

Every briefing is also data. Over HTTP you get the full result as JSON with its trace. Over MCP, seven tools return each finding with its figures, its source URL and the date of its data, plus the list of sources that did not answer. No language model is needed.
